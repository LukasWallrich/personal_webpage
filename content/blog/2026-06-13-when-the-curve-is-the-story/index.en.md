> *Part of a series of AI-assisted replications, co-drafted with Claude and checked by me; see the [first post](/blog/significant-here-not-there/) for the rationale, and the [OSF project](https://osf.io/3jz8k/) for code and data.*

A 2024 *Scientometrics* paper by Yang et al. {{< cite "-Yang2024" >}} made two striking claims about how the age structure of a research team shapes what it produces. Drawing on roughly 30 million papers across 16 fields and six decades, they reported that team age diversity has an inverted-U relationship with scientific impact (teams with moderate age diversity get cited most) and a U-shaped relationship with disruption (teams at the extremes, very uniform or very spread out in age, produce the most disruptive work). Two opposite curves from one variable is the kind of result that is either deep or fragile, so we tried to break it. It bent in an instructive way.

## The reanalysis

SciSciNet, the dataset used by Yang et al., is an unrivalled resource. So we built a close reconstruction of Yang et al.'s analysis on the same underlying dataset, over their **1950–2017** window, their **16 fields**, and their restriction to **journal research articles**, about **28 million papers** (against their 30.1 million), with disruption modelled as the percentile rank of the Wu–Wang–Evans CD index {{< cite "-Wu2019" >}} as they do. It is a reconstruction, not a carbon copy: we use a leaner set of controls than they do (mean career age, team size, reference count, and year fixed effects, where they add reference age, prior publications, funding, and institution and country counts); we require a paper to have a disruption score and a field label; and we drop single-author papers (age spread is undefined for one author) and teams above 50 members (the top 0.05%, excluded only for computational tractability). None of these is the point of the exercise. The thing we deliberately change, and the thing to watch, is the *measure* of age diversity.

Why change the measure? Yang et al. gauge age diversity with the Gini coefficient of academic age and report *one minus* Gini as "diversity," and that choice has two problems. The first is orientation. The Gini coefficient they write down is the standard one, which is zero when every team member is the same age and *rises* with dispersion, so "one minus Gini" *falls* with dispersion, and what they label "diversity" is operationally a measure of age *homogeneity*; their prose compounds the confusion by describing a higher Gini as *lower* diversity, the reverse of what their own equation computes. Because an inverted-U is symmetric about its midpoint this doesn't flip their headline, but the headline variable is mislabelled. The second concerns which *kind* of diversity is meant. In Harrison and Klein's {{< cite "-HarrisonKlein2007" >}} typology the Gini coefficient is the textbook index of *disparity* (vertical inequality, the way it is used for income), whereas "team members differ in age," read as horizontal spread on an interval scale, is *separation*, whose natural index is the standard deviation. Which conception is right is not obvious: career age can also stand for seniority, status, or accumulated resources, and on that reading a disparity index is defensible. Yang et al. do not make a specific argument, and we are generally more interested in interpersonal dynamics, so we test the *separation* conception (that what matters is how spread out a team is in career stage). The standard deviation is the measure that operationalises it, correctly oriented and free of the labelling problem above. Career age is years since an author's first recorded publication, floored at 1 and capped at 60 as Yang et al. do.

## The curves reproduce

We fitted a quadratic (a linear diversity term plus a squared term) for each outcome, with controls for mean career age, team size, reference count, and publication-year fixed effects. The standardised coefficients reproduce both of Yang et al.'s shapes:

- **Impact (first-five-year citations, C5):** linear β = +.098; quadratic β = −.077 (p < 10⁻³⁰⁰): an inverted-U.
- **Disruption (percentile of the CD index):** linear β = −.065; quadratic β = +.057 (p < 10⁻³⁰⁰): a U-shape.

Both signs match Yang et al., under a cleaner and correctly-oriented measure, and the curvature survives their field control (within-field quadratic β = −.074 for impact, +.055 for disruption) and even fixed effects for the individual *journal*, a far stricter within-venue comparison (quadratic β = −.033 and +.030). On its face this is a clean reproduction. The interesting part is what happens when you ask whether it is really about *diversity*.

## You have to fit the curve to see the curve

First, a methodological aside. The story lives entirely in the squared term. A purely linear analysis would report that age diversity *helps* citations (+.049) and *reduces* disruption (−.029), and would miss that both relationships turn. And almost nobody fits the curve: in a comprehensive meta-analysis of the diversity–team-performance literature, only 33 of 534 reports tested for *any* non-linear relationship, and just one tested correctly for a turning point rather than reading a U off a significant squared term {{< cite "Wallrich2024" >}}.

A significant squared term is not itself proof the slope reverses; it could equally reflect diminishing returns. So we ran Simonsohn's {{< cite "-Simonsohn2018" >}} two-lines test with his Robin Hood breakpoint procedure (a penalised spline locates the flat region; the breakpoint is then placed algorithmically to give the weaker of the two lines the most power). For impact the test confirms two significantly opposite slopes: positive at low spread, negative at high; disruption shows the mirror image. The breakpoint itself, as Simonsohn stresses, carries no interpretable meaning. But the smooth that locates it does: its fitted maximum sits at a career-age spread of about nine years (the 62nd percentile, near the middle of the data), not out among the most age-diverse teams (the 93rd percentile), where the bare quadratic, forced to fit one symmetric parabola, had placed the vertex. A significant squared term tells you a curve exists; the two-lines test confirms the slope genuinely reverses, and the smooth suggests it does so mid-range rather than at the edge. So the curve is real. The question is what it is made of.

## Is it age diversity — or just age?

Here is the catch. Career age cannot go below 1, so a team with a low mean career age *cannot* have a high spread: the standard deviation is mechanically coupled to the mean. In our data the two correlate +.73. And Yang et al. (like almost everyone who controls for a confound by adding it to a regression) control for mean team age only *linearly*. That is not enough when the confound is this tightly and non-linearly bound to the predictor.

So we asked the cleaner question directly: among teams of essentially the *same* mean career age, does age spread still trace these curves? Absorbing integer mean-age bins as fixed effects (comparing only teams whose members are, on average, the same age), both curves collapse toward zero:

- **Impact:** quadratic β = −.077 → **−.002** (about forty times smaller).
- **Disruption:** quadratic β = +.057 → **+.010** (about six times smaller).

Net of mean career age, team age diversity has at most a negligible association with either outcome. And the sensitivity is strongly asymmetric. Reverse the test (hold *spread* fixed by absorbing SD bins and ask whether the mean-age curve survives), and it barely moves: impact's mean-age inverted-U goes from β = −.104 only to −.070, disruption's from +.073 to +.050. Mean age statistically accounts for almost all of the spread association, while spread accounts for little of the mean-age association; put both quadratics in one model and mean age dominates (for impact, β = −.083 for mean² versus −.036 for spread²). The two are not interchangeable: it is the spread relationship that is explained away by age level, not the reverse.

Before leaning on that, one feature of the data cuts both ways. Because career age is bounded, the mean does not merely correlate with the spread, it *caps* it: a team averaging two years cannot have members decades apart, so the feasible SD is itself an inverted-U in the mean, near zero at either bound. Integer mean-age bins might therefore be too coarse: within a one-year bin the mean still varies, and because spread tracks the mean so tightly, that leftover variation carries spread along with it — so some mean-age confounding survives inside each bin, and a spurious spread effect could leak through. So we tightened the adjustment progressively (half-year bins, quarter-year bins, a smooth cubic), and the two outcomes settle in different places. Impact's squared term converges to essentially zero (−.002 at one-year bins, −.0005 at a half-year, +.0002 at a quarter-year); disruption's stops shrinking and stabilises near +.01 (+.010, +.009, +.008; +.013 under the cubic). It does so on the raw CD index too, not only the percentile rank, so it is not just an artefact of disruption being rank-transformed. But there is a cost to conditioning this tightly. It narrows the range of spread left within each cell, and because a team's age spread is itself measured with noise (it is estimated from a handful of people), shrinking the real variation while that noise stays fixed drags the coefficient toward zero. So these within-bin estimates are attenuated: the surviving disruption coefficient marks the lower end of a small effect, not its exact size.

So the defensible reading is not a clean decomposition, but it highlights an important caveat to Yang et al.'s headline claim: most of the apparent spread relationship is *statistically explained* by mean career age, and once age level is flexibly held constant the association between age spread and either outcome is at most tiny. In interpretable terms, among teams within a narrow band of mean career age (nine to eleven years), predicted five-year citations peak at a moderate age spread (about 9.6 citations) and fall away at both extremes — to about 8.9 citations for a team at the 90th percentile of spread, and 8.0 at the 99th. So the most age-diverse teams trail the most-cited by roughly one citation, up to about one and a half at the extreme, and even that gap shrinks toward zero as age is controlled more finely; the disruption swing is comparably small, a couple of percentile ranks. Whether what little remains reflects residual confounding, the shared constraints of two summaries of one bounded distribution, or a genuine effect of team composition, these data cannot say. What they do show is that the headline inverted-U and U-shape are, to a first approximation, the well-documented mean-team-age patterns (which Yang et al. themselves report) reappearing through the .73 correlation between spread and level.

One last test pins down what "diversity" has to mean. We measured spread as the standard deviation of career age — absolute years apart — which is exactly the quantity the age floor ties to the mean. Re-run the de-confounding on *relative* dispersion instead — the coefficient of variation, or the Gini coefficient that is Yang et al.'s own measure — and the collapse is only partial: net of mean age a small curve survives, stable across bin widths. These measures barely move with mean age (r ≈ .1, against .73 for the SD), because dividing through by the mean strips out the scale the age floor imposes, so the mechanical coupling that sank the SD curve never takes hold. In citations the survivor is slight: holding mean career age fixed, the most-cited teams lead the most age-unequal by under half a citation at the 90th percentile of dispersion, around one only at the very extreme, against essentially nothing for absolute spread. But the Gini is a disparity index: it counts a team aged 1 and 3 as more "diverse" than one aged 28 and 32. Yang et al. adopt it only by convention, following earlier work, whereas the rationale they give for why age diversity should help — complementary knowledge, skills and perspectives — is about *variety* of expertise, not inequality.[^hk] So the surviving signal is less a reprieve for the diversity claim than a reminder that the answer turns on what "diverse" is taken to mean: the horizontal-separation reading we did test (the standard deviation) collapses, and the inequality the Gini captures is not the dimension their theory invokes.

[^hk]: Harrison and Klein {{< cite "-HarrisonKlein2007" >}} distinguish three diversity constructs, each with its own natural index: *separation* (differences in position along a continuum; the standard deviation), *disparity* (inequality in a valued resource such as status; the Gini or coefficient of variation), and *variety* (differences in kind or expertise; Blau's index or entropy on categories). Age as a continuous variable maps naturally onto separation or disparity; the variety reading — the one closest to Yang et al.'s informational rationale — would require binning career age into stages and computing Blau's index, which we leave as an extension. The point here is narrower: their measure (the Gini) is a disparity index, matching neither the separation we test nor the variety their theory implies.

Whether these results matter depends on the question you are interested in. The controlled analysis targets the question "does age *diversity*, holding age *level* fixed, matter?", which is the *claim* Yang et al. make but did not test robustly. If instead you only care about teams *as they are actually assembled*, where diverse teams also tend to be more senior, the naive between-team estimate is the relevant one, but then you cannot separate a diversity effect from a seniority effect, and should not describe the result as being about diversity. Either way, the distinct contribution of age spread, over and above age level, is close to nothing.

## What survives, and what doesn't

For completeness, the *naive* (mean-confounded) pattern is at least stable: the inverted-U for impact appears in every publication decade and the U for disruption likewise, so neither is a quirk of one era, even though career-age censoring compresses the measure in the early decades (mean spread rises from 3.7 in the pre-1980 papers to 9.1 in the 2010s, with debut years pinned at the 1950 coverage floor for 11% of early teams versus ~1% recently). The naive impact inverted-U also weakens steadily as teams grow (β = −.092 for pairs down to −.016 for teams of 26–50). But "stable across subsamples" describes the confounded curve; it is not evidence that the curve is about diversity, and the same confound applies throughout.

## How much does the relationship actually matter?

Less than the published curves suggest — and that is the point. With twenty-eight million papers everything is significant, so the discipline has to come from effect sizes, and here they tell a consistent story: the headline curvature is modest to begin with (the citation gap above, from the least to the most age-diverse teams, is under one citation), and once you compare teams of the same average age it is essentially gone. The honest summary is that Yang et al.'s two shapes reproduce as *descriptions* (moderate-age-spread teams do get cited a little more, extreme teams disrupt a little more), but that almost all of the pattern coincides with the mean-age pattern, and the part associated with age spread itself, once age level is flexibly held constant, is negligible for both outcomes.

That is three compounding lessons in one dataset. You have to fit the curve, or you miss the shape. You have to pick the measure with care, or you mislabel and mis-orient it. And you have to adjust flexibly for the level, or you will read an age-*level* pattern as an age-*diversity* one. Overall, Yang et al.'s results are reproducible, yet not always informative with regard to the questions they set out to answer. More robust analyses suggest that age diversity considerations should probably not shape how we choose our academic collaborators.
